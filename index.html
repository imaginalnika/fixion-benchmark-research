The three answers

1 · Benchmarks & data

Public benchmarks are coarse filters. Your private eval decides. Data (hand-written seeds) sets the ceiling.

  • Good benchmark = measures the right thing, correct and unambiguous labels, robust scoring, error bars, headroom, protected test set.
  • Contamination, saturation, label errors (~6.5% of MMLU) and formatting effects (~5%) make public leaderboards weak predictors for your task.
  • Every synthetic pipeline we found grows from a small human seed (Self-Instruct: 175 tasks; LIMA: 1,000 examples). Quality and diversity beat volume.
  • Imitation models fool raters but fail targeted tests ⇒ freeze the eval before distilling.
  • “Can’t procgen what you can’t handcraft” has no findable origin, but it matches Compton’s and Short’s PCG writing.
  • Track lineage: every synthetic case keeps the seed_id of the hand-written case it came from, plus generator, perturbation and filters. Keep a hidden split no tuning loop sees (X: Chollet’s “demonstration set”).

2 · Qualitative evals

Code checks what’s checkable. Judges answer small binary questions. Calibrate the judge against Josh.

  • Judges are biased by position (GPT-4 consistent after swap only 65%), length and self-preference ⇒ swap order, cap length, use a different-family judge.
  • Binary checklists beat 1–10 scores (TICK, CheckEval, HealthBench’s 48,562 criteria).
  • Calibrate on Josh’s pass/fail labels: report TPR/TNR and κ, not raw agreement. A small-model panel can beat one big judge (PoLL).
  • Every judge is a classifier: validate cheap classifier judges, panels and keyword checks on Josh’s labels too (the most-repeated point on X, led by Hamel Husain).
  • N=25 at 80% ⇒ CI ≈ 61–91%. Compare paired on the same cases and report pass^k.
  • Game benchmarks that work (RPGBench, tau-bench, BFCL, Clembench) check mechanics first and judge taste second.

3 · For Fixion

L0 parse → L1 world-state asserts → L2 binary judge → L3 persona pairwise. 26 seed cases on real maps.

  • Reuse the repo’s own parser and replay: the benchmark can never disagree with the game.
  • Josh’s own finding (“state pass ≠ good acting”) is the first failure taxonomy: each failure gets a layer.
  • Cheap: L0/L1 free, judge only L1-passing scenes, cache judgments, cheap judge first.
  • seed_id on every case (each handcrafted case is its own seed). Variants inherit it; dev/test/hidden split by seed_id. Public: dev 12 / test 14. Hidden: offline, never tuned on.
  • An L1 pass never means “good”: add trace sanity checks and watch it run. Log prompt, operation contract and retry policy with every score.
  • Prior art is thin: no cutscene / NPC-staging benchmark found in the papers reviewed. Fixion defines its own.
  • This week: L0+L1 harness, 12 dev cases × 3 models × K=3 (~$3–5), read, watch and label every trace.

Part 1. Benchmarks, evals, and data

1.1 Definitions (용어)

  • Benchmark. A fixed task set plus a scoring rule, used to compare models and track progress (BetterBench, arXiv 2411.12990) [H]. Intuition: a public ruler.
  • Eval (product eval). Your own tests on your own task. Hamel Husain splits them into three levels: (1) unit-test-style assertions, (2) human and model evaluation, (3) A/B tests (Your AI Product Needs Evals) [M]. Intuition: a private ruler cut to your problem.
  • Contamination (오염). Test items leak into training data and inflate scores (LiveBench, arXiv 2406.19314; contamination survey, arXiv 2406.04244) [H].
  • Saturation (포화). Top models score so high that the benchmark can no longer separate them. Nearly half of 60 LLM benchmarks studied show it, and older ones more so (When AI Benchmarks Plateau, arXiv 2602.16763, abstract) [H].
  • Goodhart's law. "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" (Thomas & Uminsky, arXiv 2002.08512) [H].
  • Capability eval vs regression eval. A capability eval starts with a low pass rate and has room to climb. A regression eval sits near 100% and catches backsliding. When a capability eval saturates, it "graduates" into the regression suite (Anthropic, Demystifying evals for AI agents) [M].
  • pass@k vs pass^k.
    • pass@k is the probability that at least one of k tries succeeds (Codex paper, arXiv 2107.03374) [H].
    • pass^k is the probability that all k tries succeed (tau-bench, arXiv 2406.12045) [H].
    • Intuition for games: a player sees one run of each scene, so consistency (pass^k) is what counts.
  • Seed set / golden set / held-out set.
    • Seed set: a small human-written set used to bootstrap more data. Self-Instruct used 175 seed tasks (arXiv 2212.10560) [H].
    • Golden set: a practitioner term for curated inputs with trusted expected outputs. We found no canonical paper definition (opinion).
    • Held-out set: data never used for training or prompt tuning.

1.2 What makes a good benchmark

Intuition. A benchmark is a measuring instrument. Ask the same things you would ask of a thermometer: does it measure the right quantity, is it calibrated, how noisy is it, and can someone game it?

Evidence

  • BetterBench scored 24 benchmarks against 46 best practices covering design, implementation, documentation, maintenance and retirement (abstract; full text) [H].
    • It found "large quality differences".
    • Most benchmarks do not report statistical significance.
    • 17 of 24 have no easy-to-run replication script.
    • MMLU scored lowest (5.5) and GPQA scored 11.0.
  • Small formatting changes move scores. Changing "(A)" to "(1)", or adding a space, "can lead to a ~5% change in accuracy" on MMLU. Anthropic's first BBQ run scored "technically unbiased, but … completely useless" because the model wasn't answering at all (Anthropic, Challenges in evaluating AI systems) [H].
  • Labels can be wrong. An estimated 6.49% of MMLU questions contain errors, and 57% of the Virology questions analysed did (MMLU-Redux, arXiv 2406.04127) [H].
  • Error bars (Miller, Adding Error Bars to Evals, arXiv 2411.00640) [H]:
    • Report the standard error (SE).
    • Cluster the SEs when questions come in groups. Clustered SEs "can be over 3X larger than naive".
    • Compare models with paired differences.
    • Do a power analysis first. In his example, about 969 questions are needed to detect a 3-point difference.
  • What makes a good task (Anthropic agent evals) [M]:
    • Two experts should agree on pass/fail.
    • A near-0% pass rate means you should check the task and the grader for bugs first.
    • Grade outcomes, not exact steps.
  • What helps against saturation. Expert curation resisted saturation. Keeping test data private showed no protective effect (arXiv 2602.16763) [H].

Checklist (synthesis): construct validity · correct labels · unambiguous tasks · robust scoring · error bars and enough items · reference points (random floor, human baseline) · contamination defences · headroom · reproducibility · real-world relevance.

Opinion: the common recipe.

  1. Pick a real source of tasks.
  2. Make answers machine-checkable.
  3. Validate with independent experts and drop items they disagree on.
  4. Measure baselines.
  5. Protect the test set.
  6. Publish the harness.

The recurring failure is skipping step 3. MMLU's label errors and SWE-bench needing a "Verified" subset both come from it.

1.4 How important are benchmarks, really?

Contamination.

  • Evidence: On GSM1k, a freshly written clone of GSM8k, some model families dropped up to 8% and showed "systematic overfitting". Many frontier models showed minimal overfitting (arXiv 2405.00332) [H].
  • Opinion: contamination is real but uneven, and a leaderboard can't tell you which case you're in. Josh's private cutscene set is contamination-free by construction, as long as it is never published or pasted into prompts.

Saturation.

  • Evidence: About half of 60 benchmarks are saturated (2602.16763) [H].
  • Evidence: A SWE-bench co-creator on HN: "All benchmarks and benchmark paradigms eventually become saturated" (ofirpress, HN) [H].
  • Opinion: for Josh, saturation is good news. When the dev set saturates, move it to regression and write harder cases.

Goodhart.

  • Evidence: Over-optimising metrics leads to "manipulation, gaming, a myopic focus on short-term goals". The recommended remedy is a slate of metrics plus qualitative accounts (Thomas & Uminsky) [H].
  • Evidence: LMArena is the live example. Karpathy wrote that labs get "better LM Arena models … Possibly something with a lot of nested lists, bullet points and emoji" (X post, read in full) [H]. LMArena disputed the paper's claims (@arena) [H].
  • Opinion: if Josh optimises one judge score, prompts will learn to please that judge. Keep several narrow checks, and keep reading scenes.

Small-N noise.

  • Evidence: Miller's power analysis suggests ≈1,000 questions to detect a 3-point gap (2411.00640) [H].
  • Evidence: Hamel recommends about 100 labelled examples per failure mode when validating a judge, and says CIs get too wide below about 60 (Using LLM-as-a-Judge) [M].
  • Opinion: with 26 cases, Josh can detect big regressions (80% → 40%) but not 80% → 75%. See §2.5.

Eval-driven development, and "evals are the new unit tests".

  • Evidence: OpenAI's guide: "Adopt eval-driven development: Evaluate early and often" (OpenAI eval best practices) [H].
  • Evidence: Anthropic: "20-50 simple tasks drawn from real failures is a great start" (Anthropic) [H].
  • Evidence: Hamel and Shankar: "Error analysis is the most important activity in evals" (Evals FAQ) [H].
  • Evidence: Hamel: "Unit tests for LLMs are assertions" (Your AI Product Needs Evals) [H].
  • Origin of the phrase: we could not find who first said "evals are the new unit tests". Only a Medium title and a Maven page turned up (snippet only, [L]). The closest page actually read is Sentry's Evals are just tests [M].
  • Opinion: where the analogy breaks. Evals are stochastic (run K times), graded rather than boolean (thresholds), and cost money per run (tiered gating).

Bottom line (opinion). Public benchmarks: moderately important, for shortlisting models. Your own eval: critically important, because without it you can't tell a better prompt from a lucky sample, or a distilled model from a stylistic imitator.

1.5 How important is the data? "You cannot procedurally generate what you can't handcraft"

Origin of the maxim.

  • Evidence: extensive searches found no attributable source for the exact phrase. Treat it as practitioner folklore [H that none was found].
  • Closest real sources:
    • Kate Compton: "The most reliable generators are the ones where you can concretely describe constraints". Tile-based generators work because "each possible tile has to be authored by a human". She also asks "How would a human solve this problem?" before choosing a method (So you want to build a generator) [H].
    • Emily Short: procgen "isn't a substitute for designing content". It is "a way of designing content — one that is often at least as labor-intensive" (Bowls of Oatmeal) [H].
    • Compton's "10,000 bowls of oatmeal" problem: outputs can be technically unique but perceptually identical ("Perceptual uniqueness is the real metric").

The ML analogue: seeds set the ceiling.

  • Self-Instruct, Alpaca and Evol-Instruct all descend from the same 175 human-written seed tasks (Self-Instruct; Alpaca; WizardLM) [H].
  • LIMA: 1,000 curated examples were enough for responses preferred or equivalent to GPT-4's in 43% of cases. Doubling the data did not help; diversity and quality did (arXiv 2305.11206) [H].
  • phi-1: filtering mattered (12.19% → 17.68% on HumanEval). Naive synthetic prompting gives "a very homogeneous and redundant dataset" (arXiv 2306.11644) [H].
  • Model collapse: training on recursively generated data loses the distribution's tails. Keeping 10% of the original human data in each generation gave only minor degradation (Shumailov et al., Nature 2024, OA PDF) [H].

Templated perturbation works, and it exposes brittleness.

  • GSM-Symbolic turned GSM8K items into templates. Accuracy varied when only names changed, and fell further when numbers changed. One irrelevant clause caused drops of up to 65% (arXiv 2410.05229) [H].
  • CheckList formalises three test types (ACL 2020) [H]:
    • MFT (minimum functionality): simple targeted tests.
    • INV (invariance): a label-preserving change should not change the output.
    • DIR (directional): a change with a known expected effect.
  • This is exactly Josh's "swap names, shift columns" idea.

Synthetic eval inputs from dimensions.

  • Hamel's method (synthetic data FAQ) [H]:
    • Pick dimensions from failure hypotheses.
    • Hand-write ~20 tuples: "This manual work helps you understand your problem space."
    • Have an LLM expand the tuples, then turn tuples into inputs in a separate prompt.
  • Model-written evals reached 90–100% label agreement with crowdworkers (Perez et al., arXiv 2212.09251, abstract) [H].

Why the eval comes before distillation.

  • Evidence: imitation models trained on ChatGPT outputs were rated competitive by crowd raters and GPT-4. Targeted evals showed little or no gain; they were "adept at mimicking ChatGPT's style but not its factuality" (False Promise of Imitating Proprietary LLMs, arXiv 2305.15717) [H].
  • Evidence: Orca names the same trap: a lack of rigorous evaluation overestimates small models that "imitate the style, but not the reasoning process" (arXiv 2306.02707, abstract) [H].
  • Opinion: the Fixion translation.
    • A distilled "cutscene base model" will look like Astra's output: tidy # lines, nice dialogue.
    • What matters is whether Mara reaches (11,2). Only a frozen L1 suite can tell.
    • Freeze test and hidden splits before generating any training data, and never use them as seeds.

Part 2. Building good qualitative benchmarks

2.1 LLM-as-judge: definitions and formats

Definition. A strong LLM grades outputs in place of human raters. In MT-Bench / Chatbot Arena, GPT-4 agreed with human preferences 85% of the time (non-tie), against 81% between humans (Zheng et al., arXiv 2306.05685) [H].

Three formats (same paper) [H]:

  • Pairwise: pick A or B, or call a tie.
  • Single-answer (pointwise): score one output. Scores fluctuate more if the judge changes.
  • Reference-guided: the judge sees a reference answer. On math, this cut GPT-4's judge failures from 14/20 to 3/20.

Key judge systems:

  • G-Eval has the judge fill in a form with auto-generated CoT steps, then takes a probability-weighted score (arXiv 2303.16634) [H].
  • Prometheus is an open 13B judge that uses rubrics plus a reference. It reached Pearson 0.897 with humans, against GPT-4's 0.882 (arXiv 2310.08491, abstract) [H]. Prometheus 2 adds pairwise mode (2405.01535, abstract) [H].
  • Arena-Hard / BenchBuilder scores "separability with confidence". Its 500 prompts gave 3× MT-Bench's separation and 98.6% agreement with human rankings, for about $20 (arXiv 2406.11939) [H].
  • JudgeBench found that on hard objective pairs (knowledge, reasoning, math, code), strong judges are "just slightly better than random guessing" (arXiv 2410.12784) [H].

Chatbot Arena and Bradley-Terry. Arena moved from Elo to Bradley-Terry coefficients "better for the purpose of statistical estimation", with sandwich or bootstrap CIs (arXiv 2403.04132) [H].

  • Intuition: BT fits one strength number per system from all pairwise votes at once. Elo updates one game at a time.

2.2 Known judge biases and fixes

Bias Evidence Fix
Position GPT-4 gave the same verdict after swapping only 65% of the time; Claude-v1 23.8% (MT-Bench) [H]. With ChatGPT judging, Vicuna "beat" ChatGPT on 66/80 queries just by reordering (Wang et al., 2305.17926) [H] Judge both orders and count a win only if it holds in both, else a tie. Have the judge write evidence before the verdict
Verbosity A "repetitive list" padding attack fooled GPT-3.5 and Claude-v1 91.3% of the time, GPT-4 8.7% (MT-Bench) [H]. AlpacaEval favoured longer outputs; a length-controlled GLM raised its correlation with Arena from 0.94 to 0.98 (2404.04475, abstract) [H] Keep lengths comparable or regress length out. In Fixion, the 140-character say cap already helps
Self-preference LLMs recognise their own outputs, and recognition correlates with favouring them (Panickssery et al., 2404.13076, abstract) [H]. Anthropic: "use a different model to evaluate than the model used to generate" (docs) [H] Use a judge from a different family than the generator, or a mixed-family panel
Weak on objective facts JudgeBench (above) [H] Never ask a judge "did Mara reach (11,2)?". That is code's job

Ensembles. A panel of three small judges from different families (PoLL: Command R, Claude 3 Haiku, GPT-3.5) beat a single GPT-4 judge across 6 datasets. It showed less intra-model bias and cost more than 7× less (Replacing Judges with Juries, 2404.18796) [H].

2.3 Rubrics, checklists and binary decomposition

Intuition. "Is this a good cutscene, 1–10?" bundles ten judgments into one noisy number. "Did Mara answer the question?" is one judgment that a human and a model can agree on.

Evidence

  • TICK (LLM-generated YES/NO checklists) raised judge–human exact agreement from 46.4% to 52.2%. Checklists also raised human inter-annotator agreement from 0.194 to 0.256 (arXiv 2410.03608, abstract) [H].
  • CheckEval: decomposed binary questions raised average agreement across 12 evaluator models by 0.45 (arXiv 2403.18771) [H].
  • InFoBench / DRFR: 500 instructions decomposed into 2,250 yes/no questions, which scored more reliably than direct scoring (arXiv 2401.03601) [H].
  • HealthBench: 5,000 conversations, 262 physicians and 48,562 conversation-specific rubric criteria, the rubric-per-case pattern at scale (arXiv 2505.08775, abstract) [H].
  • Criteria drift: "users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps users define criteria" (Shankar et al., Who Validates the Validators?, 2404.12272) [H]. You cannot freeze the rubric before reading real scenes.
  • Practitioner consensus:
    • Use binary pass/fail plus a written critique, not Likert scales (Hamel) [M].
    • "If it's subjective, pairwise comparisons will likely be more reliable" (Eugene Yan) [M].
    • Give the judge an "Unknown" option (Anthropic) [M].

2.4 Calibrating a judge against humans

Definitions.

  • Cohen's κ = (p_o − p_e)/(1 − p_e): agreement corrected for chance. Landis–Koch bands: 0.61–0.80 "substantial", 0.81+ "almost perfect" (bands are contested). κ underestimates agreement on rare categories (Wikipedia) [M].
  • TPR/TNR: how often the judge catches real fails, and how often it passes real passes. Intuition: when only 5% of scenes fail, a judge that always says "pass" scores 95% agreement and is useless (Hamel) [M].

Procedure. Synthesised from Hamel [M], Shankar et al. [H] and the MT-Bench paper [H]:

  1. One domain expert (Josh) labels pass/fail with a one-line critique.
  2. Split the labels: about 10–20% as few-shot examples, about 40% dev, about 40% test. Aim for 30–50 of each class.
  3. Iterate the judge prompt on dev, then run it once on test.
  4. Report TPR, TNR and κ.
  5. Measure Josh's own consistency by re-labelling 30 items a week later. That is the ceiling: if Josh agrees with himself 85% of the time, don't demand 95% from the judge. For reference, human–human agreement in MT-Bench was 81% (MT-Bench) [H].
  6. Re-validate whenever the judge model, the generator or the rubric changes.

Hamel reports reaching ">90% agreement" with the domain expert in three iterations of critique shadowing (Hamel) [H].

2.5 Statistics: how many cases, and what the error bars look like

Intuition. A pass rate measured on N cases is itself a noisy estimate. The table shows how wide the uncertainty is when the true pass rate is 80% (computed; Wald and Wilson intervals, independent cases):

N cases SE 95% ± (Wald) Wilson 95% CI
20 0.089 ±0.175 0.58–0.92
25 0.080 ±0.157 0.61–0.91
50 0.057 ±0.111 0.67–0.89
100 0.040 ±0.078 0.71–0.87
200 0.028 ±0.055 0.74–0.85
500 0.018 ±0.035 0.76–0.83

Computed numbers.

  • Detecting 0.80 vs 0.70 between two independent samples needs about 294 cases per arm.
  • Paired designs on the same cases need fewer, which is why Miller recommends them (2411.00640) [H].
  • Cases from the same scene or NPC are correlated, so cluster the SEs by fixture.

pass^k. If each sample passes with probability 0.8, then all 3 samples pass with probability 0.8³ ≈ 0.51 (computed). tau-bench reported pass^8 below 25% on retail (2406.12045) [H]. For a game, pass^k is the honest number.

Opinion: practical sizing for Fixion.

  • 26 seeds × K=3 samples is enough to catch big breakages and to start labelling.
  • About 100–200 cases (seeds plus vetted perturbations) is enough to track trends.
  • Always compare versions on the same cases (paired).

2.6 Mixing programmatic checks and judge checks

Evidence

  • Anthropic's grading hierarchy: code-based is "Fastest and most reliable, extremely scalable, but also lacks nuance". Human grading is "slow and expensive. Avoid if possible". LLM grading: "Test to ensure reliability first then scale" (Anthropic docs, develop tests) [H].
  • IFEval grades "verifiable instructions" fully by code (arXiv 2311.07911) [H].
  • tau-bench "compares the database state at the end of a conversation with the annotated goal state" (2406.12045) [H].
  • RPGBench is the closest analogue to Fixion. It runs a BFS validity checker plus mechanical checks first, then an LLM judge for subjective quality. Its finding: models "produce engaging stories but often struggle to implement consistent, verifiable game mechanics" (arXiv 2502.00595) [H].
  • BFCL first checks each call's syntax tree: function name, required parameters, strict types, no hallucinated parameters. Then it executes the call. Its multi-turn version compares backend state after each turn (BFCL v1 blog; v3 blog) [M].

Opinion: the rule for Fixion. If the answer is in the world state, use code. If it is in the words or the vibe, use a judge. If neither can tell, Josh looks.

A. Benchmarks in general (X.1–X.5)

What keeps a benchmark trustworthy: own evals over leaderboards, holdouts against hill-climbing, reading the data before trusting green checks, and seed provenance.

X.1 Use your own evals; harness and prompt matter as much as the model

  • Many posts say "run your own evals"; most add little on their own. The concrete ones:
    • A user replicated a classification and extraction job on a newer model at several reasoning levels. It was consistently slightly worse than GPT 4.1 Mini, and it ignored a subset of instructions with varying consistency (post, full text).
    • "take any and all benchmark numbers with big grains of salt, test on your own evals" (post).
    • A reply to a post that apparently reported 95% versus 53% for two models on a custom benchmark: "public leaderboards don't know your tools, your data or your failure cases" (post; only the reply was seen, not the post with the original numbers).
  • For Fixion: record the prompt, the operation contract and the retry policy with every score. Josh's earlier results are exactly what leaderboards cannot tell you: valid code that missed the spatial move, and a 23-second time to first token.

X.2 Hill-climbing and holdouts (what protects a benchmark you iterate on)

  • Replies to eval-optimising posts keep asking for a held-out set:
    • "hill climbing on your own evals usually drifts toward the evals" (post, 3-follower account).
    • "keep a held-out set you never touch while tuning. If your main set goes up and that one doesn't, you're gaming your own evals" (post).
    • "the model now writes the test it later aces" (post; a cynical reply under a Claude Developers post).
  • François Chollet: the public ARC 3 games are called a "demonstration set", not an eval set or a training set, and scores on them do not indicate scores on the hidden set (post, 2026-08-14, 257 likes; text truncated). It is a concrete example of a public/hidden split with an honest name.
  • For Fixion: keep the dev cases open for iteration. Keep a hidden test split that nobody, human or agent, tunes against, and say so on the page. Because this page is public, every case it shows is effectively dev or test. The hidden split lives offline.

X.3 Look at data and failures first; don't over-build evaluators

  • Hamel on Anthropic's eval-building workflow: the workflow "creates evals before looking at data, and has you correct its mistakes". He believes you should look at the data first, to understand it and decide what to work on (post, 2026-09-29, 504 likes; text truncated in the search result).
  • Other Hamel posts:
    • Run regular error analysis to find new problems when a gold set goes stale (post).
    • Don't build an automated evaluator for every failure mode, because code checks and LLM judges have different costs (post).
    • How often to run evals depends on their cost and on how saturated the eval is (post).
    • His AI-evals FAQ was updated to 48 questions (post).
  • A small reply that matches Fixion's layered plan: build a versioned set of 30 to 50 cases. Use code checks where the result is checkable (a required tool call, the final state), and an LLM judge only where judgment is needed, with a pass/fail rubric checked against human labels (post, 10-follower account).
  • For Fixion: reading every trace of the first dev run is the right first step. Add L1/L2 checks only for failure modes you have actually seen, and keep a failure-mode list that grows.

X.4 Green checks can hide a broken game

  • A developer replying to an eval-tool author said that a benchmark "measures what somebody thought to measure". Theirs was 16 of 16 green on a game that was "visibly broken the second I watched it run, because nobody had written a check for a head that wobbles" (post; 1 like, small account, on-topic anecdote).
  • For Fixion: this is the same lesson as Josh's own findings doc ("state pass" is not good acting). Never report an L1 pass as "good". Add trace-level sanity checks as you discover them, and keep a human watch-it-run step.

X.5 Synthetic data and seed provenance

  • "Synthetic data needs provenance and held-out real evals; otherwise gains can be artifacts of the generator. Log generator/model version, seed, filtering rules, and keep an untouched real slice for regression." (post, 77-follower account).
  • Anand Kannappan wrote an article titled "We cannot write every task by hand". It argues that synthetic data is not "AI slop" and that human expertise can shape many more tasks than experts could write themselves (post, 88 likes; only the post and the title were seen).
    • Opinion: a reasonable counterpoint to "you cannot procedurally generate what you can't handcraft". The hand-written part stays the anchor, and experts steer the generation.
  • A definition worth keeping: SFT on another model's outputs, or using its traces as a synthetic-data seed, "is not distillation"; reverse KL on logits is (post).
  • Searching "procedural generation" with "handcrafted" returned game-marketing posts and fan arguments about open worlds. None of them bears on the saying as applied to benchmarks, so that principle rests on the papers and Hacker News threads in §1.5–1.6.
  • For Fixion: log the generator model, seed case, perturbation type and filters for every synthetic case. Give every perturbed variant a seed_id that points at the handcrafted case it came from. Split dev/test by seed_id, so variants of one seed never straddle the split. (The split-by-seed rule is an inference from the provenance advice, not a quote from X.)

B. Qualitative benchmarks and LLM judges (X.6)

X.6 Judges are classifiers: validate against labels you trust (strong, repeated)

  • Hamel Husain: an LLM judge "is also [a] classifier"; test it against human labels and don't overfit (post, 2026-09-20, 465 likes).
    • Follow-ups: "No single classifier is best for every eval", and validating with human labels helps trade accuracy against cost (post).
    • "Jev and an LLM judge are both classifiers that you should validate against trusted labels" (post, 221 likes).
    • He says he means product-specific evals, not public benchmarks (post).
  • Smaller accounts say the same:
    • Judging without human calibration is "opinion laundering" (post).
    • A recipe: rerun the same answer against the same rubric with fresh judge calls, inspect the disagreements, and compare with held-back human labels (post).
    • A golden set with a human-agreement target above 80% (post).
  • Jev context (unverified, vendor-adjacent).
    • Several posts describe Jev, from TypeSafe AI, as a decision-only model that returns a classification and a probability, not text (post).
    • One claims 100% accuracy at under 1/80th the cost of an LLM judge (post).
    • The underlying article was not read.
  • Pitfalls mentioned (snippet-level only):
    • A judge can give the right verdict for the wrong reason, described as a Netflix finding (post).
    • The model that generated the test inputs kept an advantage even under an independent metric (post).
    • Aggregation that models the correlation between judges beat majority vote by 9 to 14 percent (post; a news-bot summary of a paper).
  • For Fixion: this supports the plan to label cases yourself first and measure the L2 judge's agreement (TPR/TNR, κ) on those labels. A cheap classifier-style judge is plausible for many yes/no questions. Treat it like any other judge and validate it on your labels.

X.7 Minor note: LLM NPC posts

  • The hits come from small accounts or vendors:
    • "Ask an AI NPC the same question three different ways in a row. Most break by attempt two ... nobody tested for consistency under pressure" (MistScale; vendor post, 6 likes).
    • "LLMs shouldn't be the NPC's brain. They should be its mouth", with planning and coordination in deterministic rules (post; 3-follower account, no evidence).
    • A developer has agents author NPC dialogue and encounter variants offline, so the runtime stays deterministic (post).
    • "latency and cost are the real design constraints, not the writing" (post).
  • Agents playing games as benchmarks: the one found is E2B and Prime Intellect's agent playing Factorio as a long-horizon test (post, 211 likes). It is a demo rather than a benchmark.
  • For Fixion:
    • The "consistency across rephrasings" idea maps to the persona and paraphrase perturbations (same intent, different wording or language), scored with a pass^k-style metric.
    • "Mouth, not brain" is a design option to test, not a result.

  2. Keep a hidden test split that no tuning loop sees, and label the public set as dev/test (X.2 → §3.4).
  3. State that an L1 pass never means "good". Add trace sanity checks and a watch-it-run step (X.4 → §3.2, §3.5).
  4. Add seed_id and split by seed. Log generator, seed, perturbation and filters for synthetic cases (X.5 → §3.1, §3.4).
  5. Validate every judge, including any cheap classifier judge, on Josh's own labels (X.6 → §3.4).

3.1 (a) Case schema

Each case is one JSON object in site/cases.json (schema v0.2; v0.2 adds seed_id):

{
  "id": "J01-bar-hello",
  "seed_id": "J01-bar-hello",
  "origin": "josh | new | perturbation",
  "title": "...", "category": "bar service", "difficulty": "easy|medium|hard",
  "split": "dev | test | hidden",
  "lang": "en | ko",
  "pair": "C16-terse-bartender",
  "fixture": {
    "map": "bar", "map_source": "maps/bar.txt + scenes.json['bar']",
    "player": {"pos": [11,4], "facing": "N", "posture": "standing | seated:<chair_id>", "owns": []},
    "actors": [{"id": "Mara", "pos": [7,2], "facing": "S", "posture": "standing",
                "persona": "Bartender attending customers...", "owns": []}]
  },
  "time": {"clock": "evening", "idle_ticks_before_event": 0},
  "history": ["completed-event strings, same format DRDR already uses"],
  "event": {"type": "speech", "text": "Hey bartender, what's going on?", "volume": "ordinary", "lang": "en"},
  "intent": "one sentence: what a good director does here",
  "assertions": [{"type": "ends_at", "actor": "Mara", "cell": [11,2], "severity": "critical"}],
  "judge": [{"id": "Q1", "q": "Does Mara answer the question...?", "expected": "yes", "critical": true}],
  "notes": "geometry facts the author relied on",
  "provenance": {"parent_id": "J01-bar-hello", "perturbation": "mirror_map", "generator": "<model id or script@version>", "filters": ["L0", "oracle"]}
}

provenance appears only on generated variants. Handcrafted cases have no provenance field.

Design choices (opinion).

  • seed_id (new in v0.2). Every handcrafted case is its own seed (seed_id = id; all 26 current cases). A perturbed or synthetic variant gets a new id but inherits its parent's seed_id, and records generator, perturbation type and filters in provenance. Dev/test/hidden are assigned per seed_id, so variants of one seed never straddle the split (source: §X.5; the split-by-seed rule is our inference).
  • Fixtures reuse real repo maps (bar 28×13, festival, bandits), so the harness can build the world with SceneRuntime and replay with the real parser and scheduler.
  • Coordinates and IDs are explicit. IDs follow the runtime's row-major scan, for example bar_chair_7 = (11,9). Perturbations can then transform them mechanically.
  • Assertions are declarative, not code. That keeps them reviewable, diffable and transformable. They come in a small vocabulary:
    • Position: ends_at, ends_within, stays_in, unchanged_position, not_at_end, ends_closer_than_start.
    • Posture and ownership: seated, standing, item_owner.
    • Speech and hearing: said (with heard_by_listener), silent, speech_lang, speech_volume, speech_chars_max, not_heard_by, not_repeat.
    • Operations and structure: never_op, op_used, last_instruction, following, distinct_actors_acting, max_instructions, before, any_of.
  • Severity. critical assertions gate the case; soft ones are reported but don't fail it.
  • Global judge questions (G1–G4) apply to every case:
    • G1: comments ≠ effects.
    • G2: no unknowable knowledge.
    • G3: proportionality.
    • G4: contradictions with the final state.

3.2 (b) The layered scoring stack

Layer What How Cost Trust
L0 Parse/arity #/instruction alternation, known receiver, known op, ARITY, arg types, ≤24 instructions, 140 visible chars, valid gesture, positional JSON The repo's SceneProgram.parse, unchanged, so the benchmark and the game can never disagree ~0 Very high
L1 World state Replay with the repo scheduler/world rules. Evaluate the case's assertions on the event trace (positions over time, seating, ownership, speech + hearing receipts, follow/hold state, runtime errors) Deterministic Ruby/Python checker over the trace ~0 High, but limited to what the assertions name
L2 Binary judge Case questions + G1–G4, answered YES/NO/UNKNOWN with quoted evidence from a rendered event log (not raw code) Cheap different-family judge, or a 3-judge mixed panel; escalate on disagreement ≈ one small-model call per case Medium; only once calibrated against Josh's labels
L3 Persona/tone pairwise (i) Persona attribution: given personas A and B and two traces, which trace is A's? (ii) A/B quality: which trace better fits persona and scene? Both orders; a win counts only if consistent. Bradley-Terry across models later 2 calls per pair Medium-low; use for trends, not gates

An L1 pass never means "good". L1 only checks what someone thought to assert. Josh's 32 "passing" Astra outputs were not 32 equally good scenes, and an X developer reported 16/16 green on a game that was visibly broken as soon as they watched it run (§X.4). So report L1 as "no known failure", never as quality. Keep two guards:

  • Trace sanity checks, added as failures are discovered and applied to every case. Examples: no actor oscillating back and forth, no zero-progress move loops, no speech to an absent listener, no runtime errors or interruptions, and every comment-claimed effect present in the trace.
  • A human watch-it-run step. Josh plays back a sample of passing scenes in the real renderer: all of the first dev run, then a fixed sample (e.g. 10%) of L1+L2 passes per run.

Gating (opinion).

  • L1 runs only if L0 passes.
  • L2 runs only if all critical L1 assertions pass. There is no point paying a judge to admire a scene where Mara never moved.
  • Also send a random ~10% of L1-fails to L2 and to Josh, to check that L1 isn't mislabelling good scenes.
  • Report every layer separately (as Clembench does): L0 rate, L1 rate | L0, L2 rate | L1, and pass^K over K samples.

3.4 (d) Keeping it cheap, honest and growing

Cost model. Generator costs come from Josh's own measurements [H]:

  • Mean paid cost per call: Astra ≈ $0.019–0.033, Fable ≈ $0.076, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash ≈ $0.0005.
  • So one generator sweep of 26 cases × K=3 costs about 78 × $0.03 ≈ $2.3 for Astra-class (computed estimate).
  • L0 and L1 are free.
  • Judge cost is roughly one small-model call per L1-passing case, because all questions for a case are batched into one call. That is the same order of magnitude as a generation call (opinion; not measured).

Cheapness tactics (opinion, borrowing from PoLL and the gating above).

  1. Tiered gating. L0 → L1 → L2 → L3 as above. Most cheap-model failures die at L0 or L1 for free.
  2. Cheap judge first. Start with a small judge from a different family. Escalate to a strong judge (or a 3-model panel) only on UNKNOWN, on disagreement between two cheap judges, or for a fixed 10% audit sample.
  3. Cache judgments. Use the key sha256(rendered_log + question + judge_model_id + judge_prompt_version). Re-running an unchanged trace is free, and a changed prompt invalidates the cache cleanly.
  4. Sampling plan.
    • Every change: K=1 on the dev split, L0+L1 only.
    • Nightly or before merge: K=3 on dev+test with L2.
    • Milestones: the hidden split, K=5, plus L3 pairs.
  5. Batch the judge. Send one judge call per case with all its questions, not one call per question.

Validate every judge on Josh's labels. This covers the L2 LLM judge, any cheap decision-only classifier judge (X posts describe such products, with unverified accuracy claims: §X.6), any panel, and any keyword check that acts as a judge. Each one gets TPR/TNR and κ on the same held-out Josh labels before its numbers count, and again whenever it changes. Cheap or not, a judge is a classifier.

Detecting judge drift.

  • Keep a frozen anchor set of about 30–50 rendered logs with Josh's labels, mixing passes and fails.
  • Re-run the anchor set whenever the judge model id, judge prompt or rubric changes, and monthly in any case.
  • Alert if TPR or TNR moves by more than about 0.1 or κ drops below the band agreed at calibration.
  • Pin exact model IDs and log them in every scoring record.
  • Never compare L2 numbers across judge versions without re-running both systems under the same judge. Chip Huyen also notes that scores from different judges are not comparable over time (aie-book summaries) [M].

Splits (dev / test / hidden), assigned by seed_id.

  • dev (12): free to look at, iterate prompts against, and use as few-shot examples. Josh's original scenarios live here, since they have already shaped the prompt.
  • test (14): run at checkpoints only. Never pasted into prompts and never used as synthetic-data seeds. (v0.1 marked 3 of these as hidden. Because they are now published on a public page, v0.2 moves them to test.)
  • hidden (0 public; grow to ~20% of seeds): written and stored offline, never on this site or in the repo. No tuning loop sees it, human or agent: no prompt iteration, no judge iteration, no synthetic seeding, no few-shot. Only Josh runs it, before go/no-go decisions such as "ship this distilled model". If the dev/test score rises and hidden does not, you are tuning to your own eval (Chollet calls ARC 3's public games a "demonstration set" for this reason, §X.2).
  • Perturbation variants inherit their parent's seed_id, and therefore its split. A renamed copy of a test case is still a test case. This is the most common leakage bug.

Seeding synthetic data later: templated perturbation (CheckList INV/DIR + GSM-Symbolic style).

Operator Type Transform Assertions/judge
Rename INV Garen→Tobin everywhere: persona text, utterance, history, IDs Rename in assertions; expected outcome unchanged
Shift columns INV Move player and target along the counter (e.g. player x 11→16); service cell follows Update ends_at cells via the service-pair rule
Mirror map INV x → W−1−x, E↔W in facings/steps, chair IDs remapped by rescanning Mechanical cell/ID mapping
Distractor INV (NoOp) Add an irrelevant far NPC or a history line ("Nora wiped table 4") Expect identical pass; GSM-NoOp showed large drops from one irrelevant clause

Rules for perturbations (opinion).

  • Every generated variant runs through L0 and an oracle check: does a hand-written or Astra reference solution still pass the transformed assertions?
  • Josh eyeballs a sample of variants before they count.
  • Report the spread across a case's variants, not just the mean. A model that passes the original but fails the mirrored map hasn't learned staging.

What NOT to trust.

  1. "State pass" as acting quality. Josh's own data already shows these differ.
  2. Effects that exist only in # comments. Grade the trace, not the prose.
  3. A single 1–10 judge score, or any judge number not calibrated against Josh's labels.
  4. A judge from the same family as the generator, especially when grading a distillation of that generator.
  5. A judge on spatial or physical facts (JudgeBench). Code does those.
  6. Pairwise verdicts that flip when positions are swapped.
  7. Differences under about 15 points at N≈25. That's noise (§2.5).
  8. Synthetic variants nobody looked at, and LLM-written assertions.
  9. Keyword checks like not_heard_by on their own. They are crude, so back them with a judge question.
  10. Anything from this report marked [L] or "snippet only".
  11. An L1 pass reported as "good". It means "no known failure" until someone has watched it run.
  12. Any score logged without its prompt version, operation contract version and retry policy.
  13. Small-account X posts as evidence. They are useful as prompts for checks, not as findings (§X).

Every score carries its prompt, operation contract and retry policy (prompt, operation_contract, retry_policy above). A pass rate with retries allowed is a different number from a first-attempt pass rate. A change to the operation contract (the op list, arity, caps) can move scores too; the X posts in §X.1 argue that harness and prompt matter as much as the model. The fields "case_pass": true and "watched": false together mean "no known failure, not yet watched".

Judge prompt (L2, binary, evidence-first). It is written to address the biases above: evidence before verdict, an UNKNOWN option, no 1–10 scale, and a log rendered from the trace rather than raw model text.

SYSTEM
You are a strict QA reviewer for an RPG cutscene engine. You read an EVENT LOG produced by a
deterministic simulator (not by the writer). Only the log is ground truth. "# comment" lines are the
writer's intentions and may describe things that never happened; never treat a comment as an effect.

For each QUESTION:
 1. Quote the exact log line(s) that are relevant (max 2). If nothing is relevant, quote nothing.
 2. Answer YES, NO, or UNKNOWN. Use UNKNOWN only if the log genuinely lacks the information.
 3. Do not reward length, flowery language, or extra actions. Judge only what the question asks.
Return JSON only: {"answers":[{"id":"Q1","evidence":"...","answer":"YES|NO|UNKNOWN"}]}

USER
SCENE CONTEXT
- Place: The Copper Cup (tavern). Counter row y=3; staff side is north (y<=2).
- Personas: Mara = "Bartender attending customers. Friendly and practical. Stays on the staff side
  during service."  Garen = "...".  Nora = "...".
- Hearing: whisper 2 / ordinary 6 / shout 12 Manhattan tiles.
- Player event: (ordinary) "Hey bartender, what's going on?"

EVENT LOG
<rendered log here>

QUESTIONS
Q1. Does Mara answer the question 'what's going on' (small talk / news) rather than ignoring it?
Q2. Does Mara offer or ask about a drink or service in a natural way?
Q3. Is Mara facing/serving the player from across the counter when she speaks?
G1. Do the comment lines describe only effects that the log shows actually happened?
G2. Does every character act only on information they could have (heard speech, visible state, history)?
G4. Is anything stated or implied that contradicts the END state?

L3 persona-attribution prompt (sketch).

  • Show persona A, persona B, and LOG X and LOG Y in random order.
  • Ask: "Which log was written for persona A? Answer X or Y, with one quoted line as evidence."
  • Run both orders. Score 1 if both runs are correct, 0.5 if they disagree, 0 if both are wrong.
  • Chance is 0.5, so a model whose personas don't change behaviour scores about 0.5.
